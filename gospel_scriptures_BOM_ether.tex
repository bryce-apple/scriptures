			\footnote{%
				% \label{}%
				If you look back at the previous chapter and the brother's experiences with the Lord, in the first chapter we find that the brother prays three times, but each time he is prompted to do so by Jared---all three times Jared urges him to pray about something. He does end up praying all three times, but in each case it's Jared that instigates the prayer. In 1:42, in response to the third prayer, the Lord says that the people need to leave and that he'll meet them in the valley. When they get into the valley (2:4), the Lord shows up and meets them, like he promised. He then leads them through the wilderness and across various bodies of water (2:5--7), sometimes giving them directions from the cloud as though he's pointing out the way or telling them to go this way, and not that. But then they get to the beach, and they stay there for four years (2:13); after those four years the Lord shows up again, in this verse, and ``chastens'' the brother because he's not been calling on the Lord. 
				So when you put all these events together, I see the brother as a guy who was just very used to taking care of his own problems, taking care of his own family, and running things in his way, without depending much on the Lord. And because he runs things well, it's easy for him to forget to pray, because it just doesn't occur to him to do it; the need doesn't occur to him. He doesn't feel the need or lack of anything, which is why Jared is always prompting him and urging him to pray---Jared perceives the needs and opportunities and his brother does not. Just because we don't feel the lack doesn't mean the lack isn't there.
				I think this also gives us a new way of thinking about the problem that the brother has to solve with the boats at the beach. The brother and everyone else on the journey already have a bunch of experience building boats, so the Lord tells him, just build them in the way you've been building them (2:16). But then there are the problems with the air and light. The Lord tells him how to solve the air problem (2:20), but leaves it up to him to solve the light problem (2:22--25). And I think he does this partially because he is trying to help the brother grow past where he had been before, where he had been used to not relying on the Lord---so the Lord puts him in a position where it's clear he \textit{must} rely on the Lord. The Lord is working with the brother where he's at to teach him this reliance. 
				I think it's also possible that the Lord showed them how to build boats in a particular way earlier in the trip (2:6), so that they would be forced into this problem later on the trip, when he instructed them to build the boats in the way they'd done it before. Because you have to ask, if the air and light were problems at the beach, and those beach boats were built the same way as the ones from before, why weren't the air and light a problem before? It could be that the trips were shorter, or less dangerous, or they wouldn't expect to be submerged, and so they didn't have to deal with those questions. But later they do, which puts the brother in a position where he has to rely on the Lord. 
				Another point to think about here is how this beach/boat experience of the brother is similar to and different from Nephi's experience in 1 Nephi 17--18. The Lord works with them in different ways, instructs them in different ways, and teaches them different things, because they need different things.

				\footnote{%
					% \label{}%
					We first read that they are building ``barges'' in 2:6. The word ``barge'' occurs only five times in the scriptures, all in the Book of Mormon, and it's always plural. Here are the 1828 dictionary meanings:
						\begin{compactitem}
							\item[1] A pleasure boat; a vessel or boat of state, furnished with elegant apartments, canopies and cushions, equipped with a band of rowers, and decorated with flags and streamers; used by officers and magistrates.
							\item[2] A flat-bottomed vessel of burthen, for loading and unloading ships.
						\end{compactitem}
					So it doesn't just have to be a flat-bottomed boat for carrying goods. In the OED there is a meaning \#1.a:
						\begin{compactitem}
							\item[1.a] A small seagoing vessel with sails: used spec. for one next in size above the balinger, and generally as = Ship, vessel.
						\end{compactitem}
					This use is obsolete now, it says, but was in use up through at least 1875. So during JS's time, the word ``barge'' could just mean a ``ship'' or ``vessel.'' We could then ask why the record here in Ether doesn't just use ``ship,'' and I would say that the different word is probably meant to call attention to the unusual construction of these ships here, in distinction to the ones that get mentioned before in 1 Nephi and Alma.
